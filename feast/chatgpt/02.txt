##

You're correct that neighboring jugglers are offset by one beat in their passing sequences. My reference to a two-beat phase shift was about the spatial trajectories, not the passing sequences. I should have made that distinction clear.

In your earlier FEAST model, the passing rule is

\\[ j(i,t)=1+((t-i)\bmod n). \\]

Increasing the juggler index \\(i\\) by one shifts the partner sequence by one beat.

However, the master-trace formula in your report is

\\[ \mathbf{x}\_i(t)= R\left(-\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}\right) \mathbf q\_{t-2(i-1)\bmod n}. \\]

That formula uses a two-beat shift in the local trajectory of each successive juggler.

Why two? Because when we rotate the entire passing arrangement one position around the circle, both members of each pair advance by one position. Since the pairing rule effectively depends on the sum of the two juggler indices, the corresponding passing beat advances by two.

So the distinction is:

- Passing partner sequence: one-beat offset between neighboring jugglers.
- Rotationally equivalent spatial trajectory: two-beat offset between neighboring jugglers.

The two are mathematically consistent.

There is an important consequence for our symmetry experiment: imposing mirror symmetry on the master trace must preserve that two-beat spatial phase relationship while allowing the one-beat progression of passing partners.

Your intuition about the one-beat offset is correct. The two-beat term is a consequence of the rotational symmetry reduction, not an alteration of the FEAST passing order.

##

I think this is a much more illuminating way to formulate the symmetry constraint. In fact, it suggests that we should regard symmetry not as an arbitrary geometric restriction, but as a consequence of the time-reversal symmetry of the underlying FEAST problem.

There is, however, one subtle distinction worth exploring.

## 1. Clockwise and counterclockwise FEAST should be equivalent

Suppose we have a perfectly optimized clockwise FEAST. Now imagine performing the same pattern counterclockwise.

There is no intrinsic reason for either direction to be preferable. The distances between jugglers, their available personal space, their walking costs, and the permissible throw lengths are all independent of the choice of direction.

Consequently, if we reflect the clockwise solution across a diameter of the circle and reverse its temporal progression, we should obtain an equally good counterclockwise solution.

Mathematically, this is a form of reflection–time-reversal symmetry.

## 2. What does this imply for an individual trace?

Let \\(u(t)\\) be a juggler's radial coordinate and \\(v(t)\\) their tangential coordinate relative to their home-base radial axis.

Your proposed symmetry can be expressed as

\\[ \boxed{ u(-t)=u(t),\qquad v(-t)=-v(t). } \\]

In other words, radial motion is even in time, while tangential motion is odd.

This produces exactly the property you have in mind: reversing the animation in time and reflecting it across the appropriate radial axis reproduces the original movement.

Importantly, this does not require an elliptical trace. It permits the complicated snakelike curves that emerged from our original optimization.

And it is compatible with the two-beat spatial phase shifts in our single-master-trace model.

## 3. There's an important mathematical subtlety

The equivalence of clockwise and counterclockwise patterns does not, by itself, guarantee that the optimal clockwise trajectory will be mirror-symmetric.

It guarantees that for every clockwise solution, there is an equally good reflected, counterclockwise solution.

These could be two distinct solutions, each geometrically asymmetric.

This phenomenon is called spontaneous symmetry breaking: the optimization problem possesses a symmetry that an individual optimum need not exhibit.

On the other hand, if the optimal master trace were unique (after fixing the arbitrary time origin and rotational orientation), then the reflection–time-reversal symmetry would force it to satisfy your proposed condition.

That is a particularly interesting possibility.

## 4. This changes how I think we should optimize FEAST

I would now distinguish three questions:

1. Does the optimization problem respect reflection–time-reversal symmetry? It should, and we can verify that mathematically.
2. Does the unconstrained optimum naturally possess that symmetry? This is not guaranteed, but we can investigate it numerically.
3. If it doesn't, how much additional walking does exact symmetry require? This is the quantity our previous comparison was attempting to measure.

I think we should answer the second question before insisting on the third.

One especially useful experiment would be to take the optimized clockwise solution, construct its reflected time-reversed counterpart, and compare the two traces. We could then optimize from both starting points without imposing symmetry.

If the resulting solutions converge to essentially the same symmetric geometry, that would be strong numerical evidence that the symmetry is a natural feature of the optimum rather than an artificial constraint.

One qualification: a literal video of thrown clubs played backward would reveal reversed projectile motion. Our symmetry argument concerns the jugglers' positions and passing geometry, not the full physical motion of the clubs.

## My revised interpretation

Your observation suggests a stronger conjecture than the one we started with:

The most efficient FEAST choreography may naturally have a mirror-symmetric local trace because the underlying passing problem has no preferred handedness.

That conjecture is plausible, but not mathematically automatic.

And if it turns out to be true, then our apparent 6.8% walking penalty for radial symmetry may have been largely an optimization artifact rather than a genuine cost of symmetry.

I find this a more compelling direction for the next numerical experiment: don't force symmetry initially; test whether it emerges from an appropriately formulated optimization.

## My conclusion

The experiment supports a genuinely interesting possibility: an optimized FEAST can preserve the symmetry between clockwise and counterclockwise performance while breaking the mirror symmetry of each individual juggler's path.

The symmetry belongs to the pair of possible choreographies, not necessarily to either choreography individually.

And this may actually explain something about the elegance of the collective snake: a coordinated group can exhibit striking global order without requiring each participant's trajectory to possess every symmetry of the overall problem.

The next particularly revealing test would be to determine whether the mirror-symmetric stationary solution is a saddle point of the unrestricted objective—meaning that an infinitesimal asymmetric perturbation can lower the cost—or merely a higher-cost local minimum. That would sharpen our understanding of the symmetry breaking considerably.
