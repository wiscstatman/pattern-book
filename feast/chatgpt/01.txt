## 1. The mathematics of the feast

There is a particularly nice structure to your pattern: it is a cyclic round-robin matching of the jugglers, with phantom passes included. This makes it possible to formulate the entire problem for arbitrary \\(n\\), rather than programming the eight-person case separately.

Number the passing beats \\(t=0,1,\ldots,n-1\\), and label the jugglers \\(i=1,\ldots,n\\).

On beat \\(t\\), the partner of juggler \\(i\\) is

\\[ \boxed{j(i,t)=1+((t-i)\bmod n)} \\]

When \\(j(i,t)=i\\), that juggler makes a phantom pass. Otherwise, \\(i\\) and \\(j\\) exchange clubs.

For \\(n=8\\), this gives:

| Passing beat | Actual exchanges   | Phantom passes |
| ------------ | ------------------ | -------------- |
| 1            | 1–8, 2–7, 3–6, 4–5 | None           |
| 2            | 2–8, 3–7, 4–6      | 1, 5           |
| 3            | 1–2, 3–8, 4–7, 5–6 | None           |
| 4            | 1–3, 4–8, 5–7      | 2, 6           |
| 5            | 1–4, 2–3, 5–8, 6–7 | None           |
| 6            | 1–5, 2–4, 6–8      | 3, 7           |
| 7            | 1–6, 2–5, 3–4, 7–8 | None           |
| 8            | 1–7, 2–6, 3–5      | 4, 8           |

Every juggler encounters every other juggler exactly once, plus one phantom, over the \\(n\\) beats.

An important symmetry is

\\[ j(i+k,t+2k)\equiv j(i,t)+k\pmod n. \\]

This means a spatial rotation by \\(k\\) positions corresponds to a time shift of \\(2k\\) beats. Thus your expectation of identical traces, rotated appropriately, has a strong mathematical basis, although the traces generally have different phases in time.

## 2. What should be optimized?

Let \\(\mathbf h_i\\) be juggler \\(i\\)'s home position, and let \\(\mathbf x\_{i,t}\in\mathbb R^2\\) be their position at passing beat \\(t\\).

I would start with four costs, rather than just two:

\\[ \boxed{ E=E\_{\text{home}}+E\_{\text{throws}}+ E\_{\text{movement}}+E\_{\text{collision}} } \\]

Home-position cost

\\[ E\_{\text{home}}= \alpha\sum\_{t,i}\\|\mathbf x\_{i,t}-\mathbf h_i\\|^2 \\]

This discourages wandering too far from home.

Throw-distance cost

For a passing pair \\(\\{i,j\\}\\), define \\(d\_{ij,t}=\\|\mathbf x\_{i,t}-\mathbf x\_{j,t}\\|\\).

A useful penalty is

\\[ P(d)=\beta(d-6)\_+^2+\gamma(d-12)\_+^2, \qquad (z)\_+=\max(z,0). \\]

This penalizes throws longer than 6 feet and adds a much stronger cost beyond 12 feet. Phantom passes are excluded from this term.

Movement cost

\\[ E\_{\text{movement}}= \eta\sum\_{t,i}\\|\mathbf x\_{i,t+1}-\mathbf x\_{i,t}\\|^2 \\]

with periodic indexing, so beat \\(n\\) returns smoothly to beat 1.

This is especially important in practice. Without it, the optimum might demand that jugglers jump abruptly from one passing position to another.

Collision constraint

For all distinct \\(i,j\\),

\\[ \boxed{\\|\mathbf x\_{i,t}-\mathbf x\_{j,t}\\|\geq6\text{ ft}.} \\]

Your 3-foot-radius discs imply a minimum center-to-center spacing of 6 feet.

Ideally, this is a hard constraint, not merely an extra cost. We must also check collisions between passing beats, not just at the discrete positions.

One further consideration: the mathematically shortest throw need not be the physically best throw. Eventually, we could add a facing-direction cost and a penalty for passing lanes that become awkwardly close, while keeping the no-crossing property.

## 5. Where I would take this next

There is a particularly interesting question hidden in your original conjecture: is the optimal trace a simple, smooth, closed curve, and is it necessarily identical for every juggler up to rotation and time shift?

The matching rule has the required symmetry, but an unconstrained numerical optimizer can find solutions that break that symmetry. A stronger model could explicitly enforce rotated copies of one shared trace, dramatically reducing the number of independent variables.

I would also experiment with replacing the step-to-step movement penalty with an acceleration penalty. That would favor smooth trajectories and discourage abrupt turns, which seems more realistic for people moving while juggling.

Finally, I would compare the resulting shapes as \\(n\\) increases. The geometry of an eight-person feast is quite different from that of a sixteen-person feast, particularly when the home-base separation and maximum comfortable throw distance remain fixed.

One question that would materially affect the next version: When your jugglers move closer together for a long pass, do they mostly move radially toward the center of the circle, or do they also make significant sideways movements along the perimeter? That distinction could help determine whether we should optimize unrestricted two-dimensional motion or a simpler radial-and-tangential model.

## 1. Two different notions of “smooth”

There are really two separate improvements one can make:

### A. Smooth the *drawing*
Keep the optimized discrete positions \(\mathbf x_{i,1},\dots,\mathbf x_{i,n}\), but draw each juggler’s loop using a **periodic spline** through those points. This improves the picture immediately and still shows the actual passing points.

### B. Smooth the *motion itself*
Add a curvature / acceleration penalty to the optimization:

\[
E_{\text{smooth}}
=
\kappa \sum_{t,i}
\left\|
\mathbf x_{i,t+1} - 2\mathbf x_{i,t} + \mathbf x_{i,t-1}
\right\|^2 .
\]

This is the more principled version. It penalizes “kinks” in the beat-to-beat motion and tends to produce a genuinely smoother loop, not just a prettier plot.

So my recommendation is:

- **use periodic splines for visualization**, and
- **add a second-difference penalty** if you want the optimization itself to favor smooth traces.

## 2. Radial-vs-tangential movement

Since you said jugglers naturally move more easily **in and out** than **sideways**, I’d replace the isotropic home penalty

\[
\alpha \|\mathbf x_{i,t} - \mathbf h_i\|^2
\]

with an anisotropic one.

Let \(u_i^{(r)}\) be the outward radial unit vector at home base \(i\), and \(u_i^{(\tau)}\) the tangential unit vector. If

\[
\mathbf x_{i,t} - \mathbf h_i
=
r_{i,t} u_i^{(r)} + \tau_{i,t} u_i^{(\tau)},
\]

then use

\[
E_{\text{home}}
=
\sum_{i,t}
\left(
\alpha_r r_{i,t}^2
+
\alpha_\tau \tau_{i,t}^2
\right),
\qquad
\alpha_\tau > \alpha_r.
\]

That means sideways motion is penalized more than radial motion.

This is a very natural model for what you described.

## 3. Why the tear-drop appears

The tear-drop is not an accident. It comes from an asymmetry in what the juggler is trying to achieve over the cycle:

- when the next partner is **far away**, the efficient way to shorten the throw is to move **inward** toward the center;
- when the next partner is **nearby** along the ring, the juggler does **not** need a comparably large outward move, because outward motion helps only a little and creates crowding issues.

So the loop typically has:

- a **broad, shallow outer side**, and
- a **deeper inward point** aimed toward the center.

That is exactly a tear-drop / lopsided oval.

With a radial preference, this effect gets stronger.

## 4. What I found for larger \(n\)

Using the same basic optimization idea, but with radial motion cheaper than tangential motion, I checked \(n=8,10,12\).

Qualitatively:

- **\(n=8\)**: clear tear-drop
- **\(n=10\)**: still clearly tear-drop, more elongated inward
- **\(n=12\)**: still the same basic form, but now much more stretched radially

So the answer is: **yes, the tear-drop persists**, and in fact becomes **more pronounced** for larger \(n\), at least with fixed adjacent home spacing and the same comfort thresholds.

A useful summary from one representative run (same adjacent home spacing \(=7\) ft, same personal radius \(=3\) ft):

| \(n\) | Home-circle radius (ft) | Max throw (ft) | Max displacement from home (ft) | Qualitative trace |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 8  | 9.15 | 8.57 | 4.94 | tear-drop |
| 10 | 11.33 | 9.02 | 7.20 | stronger tear-drop |
| 12 | 13.52 | 12.06 | 11.88 | strongly inward-stretched tear-drop |

Two important observations:

1. **The home circle radius grows with \(n\)** if adjacent spacing is fixed:
   \[
   R=\frac{s}{2\sin(\pi/n)} \sim \frac{s\,n}{2\pi}.
   \]
   So larger groups naturally spread out more.

2. By \(n=12\), with 7-foot adjacent spacing, the model is already pushing close to your “high penalty beyond 12 feet” throw threshold.

So for larger \(n\), one of three things has to happen:

- jugglers move **more inward**,
- the adjacent home spacing \(s\) is reduced, or
- longer throws must be accepted more often.

That is exactly the kind of phase transition worth studying.

## 5. Best modeling upgrade from here

If I were refining the model now, I would use

\[
E
=
E_{\text{throw}}
+
E_{\text{home,radial/tangential}}
+
E_{\text{movement}}
+
E_{\text{smooth}}
+
E_{\text{collision}}.
\]

Concretely:

- **throw penalty**
  \[
  \beta(d-6)_+^2+\Gamma(d-12)_+^2
  \]
- **radial/tangential home penalty**
  \[
  \alpha_r r^2+\alpha_\tau \tau^2,\quad \alpha_\tau>\alpha_r
  \]
- **movement penalty**
  \[
  \eta\|\mathbf x_{t+1}-\mathbf x_t\|^2
  \]
- **smoothness penalty**
  \[
  \kappa\|\mathbf x_{t+1}-2\mathbf x_t+\mathbf x_{t-1}\|^2
  \]
- **collision**: enforce center-to-center separation \(\ge 6\) ft

That would give you a much more realistic “human” trajectory.

## 6. R changes for smoothed traces

Here is the key idea for smoothing the plotted loops in R.

After your optimized `x[beat, juggler, xy]` array is computed, build a dense periodic spline for each juggler:

```r
dense_per_edge <- 30L

trace_list <- lapply(seq_len(n), function(j) {
  px <- x[,j,1]
  py <- x[,j,2]
  tt <- c(seq_len(n), n+1)

  fx <- splinefun(tt, c(px, px[1]), method = "periodic")
  fy <- splinefun(tt, c(py, py[1]), method = "periodic")

  phase <- seq(1, n+1, length.out = n * dense_per_edge + 1)

  data.frame(
    juggler = factor(j),
    phase = phase,
    x = fx(phase),
    y = fy(phase)
  )
})

traces_smooth <- do.call(rbind, trace_list)
```

Then plot both the smooth path and the discrete passing points:

```r
ggplot() +
  geom_path(
    data = traces_smooth,
    aes(x, y, group = juggler, color = juggler),
    linewidth = 0.9, alpha = 0.5
  ) +
  geom_point(
    data = positions,
    aes(x, y, color = juggler),
    size = 2
  ) +
  geom_point(
    data = homes,
    aes(x, y),
    shape = 3, color = "grey55", size = 3
  ) +
  coord_fixed() +
  theme_minimal()
```

That gives exactly what you asked for: **smoothed traces, with the key beat positions visibly marked**.

## 7. R change for radial preference

This is the main model change I’d make.

Suppose `home` is your matrix of home positions. Define radial and tangential unit vectors:

```r
radial_unit <- home / sqrt(rowSums(home^2))
tangent_unit <- cbind(-radial_unit[,2], radial_unit[,1])
```

Then, inside the energy function, replace the isotropic home penalty with:

```r
dx <- x - initial

radial_coord <- dx[,,1] * rep(radial_unit[,1], each = n) +
                dx[,,2] * rep(radial_unit[,2], each = n)

tang_coord   <- dx[,,1] * rep(tangent_unit[,1], each = n) +
                dx[,,2] * rep(tangent_unit[,2], each = n)

result <- result +
  alpha_r * sum(radial_coord^2) +
  alpha_t * sum(tang_coord^2)
```

with, for example,

```r
alpha_r <- 0.08
alpha_t <- 0.50
```

so that tangential drift is significantly more expensive than radial movement.

## 8. If you want smoother *actual* motion

Add this to the objective:

```r
prev_index <- c(n, 1:(n-1))
next_index <- c(2:n, 1)
accel <- x[next_index,,,drop=FALSE] - 2*x + x[prev_index,,,drop=FALSE]
result <- result + kappa * sum(accel^2)
```

with something like

```r
kappa <- 0.3
```

to start.

That term tends to suppress polygonal/kinky loops and makes the optimized trace itself more naturally rounded.

I think you've identified the right mathematical simplification. The optimization should describe one juggler's closed trajectory, not twenty independently optimized trajectories. Every other juggler should follow an exact rotated copy, with the appropriate time shift.

This enforces the rotational symmetry that made the eight-person solution so appealing.

## 1. The single-trace formulation

Let \\(\mathbf q(t)\\) be the trajectory of juggler 1, with periodicity

\\[ \mathbf q(t+n)=\mathbf q(t). \\]

Let \\(R(\theta)\\) be the usual two-dimensional rotation matrix. Then the trajectory of juggler \\(i\\) is

\\[ \boxed{ \mathbf x_i(t)= R\left(-\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}\right) \mathbf q\big(t-2(i-1)\big). } \\]

Here \\(t=0\\) is the first passing beat, and juggler 1 starts at the top of the circle after an overall rotation of the coordinate system.

The phase shift of two beats per juggler is the crucial detail. It follows directly from your passing schedule.

For \\(n=20\\), we now optimize only 20 two-dimensional points, or 40 coordinates, rather than the previous 800.

More importantly, symmetry is guaranteed by construction, not merely encouraged by the objective function.

## 3. Why this approach works better

The key change is that the optimizer cannot give one juggler an advantageous position that breaks rotational symmetry. It must improve the single master trace, which automatically improves all twenty jugglers' trajectories.

I retained the preference for radial movement, allowed more movement between passing beats, and included collision penalties sampled along the smooth trajectories themselves.

The movement result is particularly relevant to your suggestion of an 8-count feast: a maximum displacement of approximately 5 feet between passing beats is much more reasonable with eight club releases available than with four. The appropriate count will also depend on tempo and how quickly the jugglers can comfortably change position.
